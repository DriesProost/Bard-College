# The College of the Quill — Writing curriculum

**Approved educational proposal — Phase 1 implemented on 20 October 2026.** Based on selective, documented consultation of all eight supplied books and direct checks of the key visual examples used in this plan. This is not a claim to have read every page, verified every score, heard companion audio, or demonstrated learner outcomes.

Phase 1 implementation details, data migrations, achievable minimum-task adjustments and evidence semantics are documented in [CURRICULUM.md](CURRICULUM.md) and [PHASE1_VALIDATION.md](PHASE1_VALIDATION.md). This approved syllabus remains the pedagogical reference; the shipped data package contains the current explanations and criteria. Music/shared studios remain deferred.

## Recommendation

**38 lessons: the existing 30 stable IDs plus eight purposeful extensions.** Four stages contain 10, 9, 10 and 9 units. The core integrates poetry, lyrics, narrative miniatures, humour and revision. It does not claim to be a complete fiction degree or an exhaustive guide to every verse form. Sonnet/villanelle/sestina and long fiction can be later optional studies, not extra badges inserted to enlarge this release.

The stage order below is a recommended route; the listed skill dependencies are readiness requirements, not the current application's global completion chain. Existing IDs, attempts and completed work survive. New Writing exercises do not require taking the Music path. Where an existing lesson invites humming, speaking an original rhythmic phrase is an accessible alternative. Historical “Lute” tags on lyric lessons do not become Music proficiency.

## Teaching and assessment

The goal is independent creative decisions, not lesson completion. A normal session is one minute noticing an example, three minutes making or revising, and one minute explaining the choice. A unit can require several sessions. Optional 15–30-minute practice and multi-session projects are labelled honestly; completing a whole song is never a five-minute promise. The timing and sequence are our instructional design, not the books' programme reproduced in miniature. Kachulis recommends at least half an hour for a source lesson.

Use four evidence states: introduced; demonstrated with support; independently demonstrated; retained/transferred. Keep “insufficient evidence” separate from failure. A completed exercise/XP records participation. Recommend readiness after a relevant attempt and explained revision; confirm retention with a delayed unfamiliar prompt. Learners can practise or attempt a diagnostic at any level; a bypass is not mastery. No scalar AI score controls access.

Review after approximately 1, 3, 7 and 14 days, adapting to errors and learner choice. These intervals are our unvalidated starting heuristic. Each unit retrieves its listed prerequisite skills; every stage challenge combines at least two skills. Reuse saved work for focused revision, then test transfer with a different subject or musical constraint. A learner may reasonably retain an irregularity and explain it.

The GPT-6 Luna tutor receives the relevant original explanation, objective, task, criterion IDs, representation, intention, selected earlier work and evidence state. It cites a specific observation, explains its likely effect, offers one or two useful revisions and one question. It teaches without replacing the student's work, avoids excessive praise and acknowledges taste, dialect and cultural context. Offline self-assessment and revision use the same criteria. Human review remains appropriate for ambiguous judgments and stage portfolios.

Source codes below resolve to precise consulted sections in [LIBRARY_CATALOGUE.md](LIBRARY_CATALOGUE.md). **T** means prose consulted, **V** means the specified visual claim checked, **D** means our instructional design, and **U** means unverified/excluded from evidentiary claims. A source supports the named principle, not authorship or endorsement of our example, task, schedule or assessment. All examples below are original; “weaker” means weaker evidence for that task, not artistically forbidden.

## 1. Foundations

### lesson-6 — Something you can touch

**Objective:** Replace vague descriptions with sensory images.

**Principle:** Specific objects give the reader evidence. Select one telling detail instead of listing every sense.

**Original contrast:** Abstract: “The room was lonely.” Specific: “One teaspoon dried beside the sink.” Weaker evidence: “It was a nice place” supplies an evaluation but no sensory evidence.

**Five-minute task:** Describe a deserted place in three lines without lonely, empty, sad, or beautiful.

**Readiness / retrieval:** Entry; no prior skill required. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) A concrete detail places a reader in a scene. (2) The detail suggests rather than labels an emotion. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S3. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-3 — Speak before you sing

**Objective:** Use natural speech before adding rhyme.

**Principle:** Start with something a person would actually say. Read it aloud before shaping the ending. Later lessons add rhyme; today, notice whether an unusual word order serves a deliberate voice or merely sounds literary.

**Original contrast:** Stilted: “Upon the road did I depart.” Conversational: “I left before the buses started.” The second sentence places a speaker in an ordinary moment. Weaker evidence: An inverted line chosen only to secure a rhyme obscures what the speaker would say.

**Five-minute task:** Return to your deserted place. Write a two-to-four-line conversation between someone arriving and someone leaving. Keep ordinary word order; no rhyme is required yet.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The dialogue sounds like intentional speech in the writer’s voice. (2) A concrete detail or choice makes the situation understandable. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S10. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-1 — Let the rhyme arrive

**Objective:** Find natural rhymes without bending meaning.

**Principle:** Begin with what you want to say. Collect several rhyming words, then choose the one that serves that meaning. A near-rhyme can sound more natural than a strained perfect rhyme.

**Original contrast:** “Your yellow scarf is on the chair. / I leave it folded for you there.” Chair/there fits an ordinary sentence; the action suggests why the object matters. Weaker evidence: “My coffee cold did grow for thee” bends ordinary syntax for its ending.

**Five-minute task:** Write two conversational lines ending in a rhyme or near-rhyme. Start with a real object.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-3. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The rhyme can be heard in the writer’s accent. (2) Meaning stays intelligible without forced word order. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S4, F2. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-2 — A rhyme worth keeping

**Objective:** Distinguish a useful rhyme from a predictable one.

**Principle:** A rhyme earns its place when it adds information or feeling. Familiar pairs are not forbidden, but love/above rarely says enough on its own.

**Original contrast:** “The kettle clicked. I counted three. / Your empty chair looked back at me.” Three/me frames a concrete absence. Weaker evidence: A novel rhyme that changes the intended event solves sound at the expense of meaning.

**Five-minute task:** Take the couplet you wrote in “Let the rhyme arrive”. Keep its intended meaning. Change one end word and revise the other line so the rhyme adds a fresh detail, rather than merely being less common.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-1, lesson-6. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The revision adds a specific detail while preserving the intended meaning. (2) The writer can explain why the new rhyme serves the scene; novelty alone is not rewarded. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S4, P18. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-near-rhyme — Hear the rhyme distance

**Objective:** Compare exact and near rhyme by stressed sound and intention.

**Principle:** Rhyme is heard, not inferred from spelling. Keep the thought constant while testing a closer or looser sound link; terminology varies across authors.

**Original contrast:** For a line ending “light”, compare endings “night” and “life”; describe the unchanged vowel and changed final consonant in the second pair. Weaker evidence: Claiming “love/move” is a perfect rhyme because the spelling matches supplies visual rather than aural evidence.

**Five-minute task:** Revise one saved couplet with two alternative endings. Speak both and choose one for its meaning and sound.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-2. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Identifies the actual sound relationship in the learner’s accent. (2) Defends a choice without contorted syntax. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S4, F2, D17. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-4 — The pulse of a sentence

**Objective:** Hear natural word stress.

**Principle:** English alternates stronger and weaker syllables unevenly. Mark the words you naturally emphasise before imposing a beat. Accent and performance affect stress.

**Original contrast:** “I LEFT my BOOTS beSIDE the DOOR.” Capitals suggest a possible spoken pulse, not a rule. Weaker evidence: Counting eight syllables without locating word stress does not identify the pulse.

**Five-minute task:** Write two lines. Mark four natural stresses in each with capitals and read them aloud.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-3. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Marked stresses are plausible in the writer’s dialect. (2) The writer distinguishes strong beats from syllable totals. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F1. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-5 — A steady walking rhythm

**Objective:** Maintain a repeatable rhythmic pattern.

**Principle:** A consistent number of strong beats helps listeners anticipate a line. Equal syllable counts alone do not guarantee rhythm. Leave room for intentional variation.

**Original contrast:** “The rain came down upon the mill / The wheel was dark, the water still.” Read both against four slow taps. Weaker evidence: Giving every syllable equal force does not demonstrate a pattern of strong beats.

**Five-minute task:** Write a four-line verse with four strong beats per line. Read it against a tapped pulse.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-4, lesson-1. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) A repeatable strong-beat pattern can be spoken aloud. (2) Any departure is intentional and its effect is explained. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F1, P14. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-metre-variation — Expect a beat; bend it deliberately

**Objective:** Distinguish syllable count, stress, metre and deliberate variation.

**Principle:** A metrical expectation is a background against which actual speech can vary. Equal syllable totals need not share the same stress pattern.

**Original contrast:** “The gate was shut. / I stayed outside.” lets the pause carry emphasis; test it against a continuous reading. Weaker evidence: Assuming two eight-syllable lines must sound alike ignores lexical stress and phrasing.

**Five-minute task:** Mark the expected beats in two saved lines, revise one collision and explain one variation you keep.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-4, lesson-5. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Marks a defensible reading and separates expectation from speech. (2) Explains the effect of the revision or intentional exception. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F1, D19. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-7 — A useful comparison

**Objective:** Make metaphor reveal a fresh relationship.

**Principle:** A metaphor connects unlike things through a precise shared quality. Extend it only while it continues to illuminate the subject.

**Original contrast:** “His apology was a borrowed coat: warm, but never quite his.” The comparison questions sincerity. Weaker evidence: Calling trust “a shiny thing” gives no shared property to explore.

**Five-minute task:** Compare trust to an everyday object in two lines. Add one detail that makes the comparison precise.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The comparison has a precise shared quality. (2) The image clarifies the subject instead of adding decoration. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P3. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-14 — The truth in a small thing

**Objective:** Express emotion through a specific action.

**Principle:** Sincerity is not the same as maximum intensity. An ordinary action can reveal what a speaker cannot say directly.

**Original contrast:** “Dad keeps my crooked birthday card / beneath his polished workbench.” Keeping an imperfect gift suggests affection without naming it. Weaker evidence: Naming “huge sadness” without an action leaves the feeling unsupported for this task.

**Five-minute task:** Write three lines of affection, gratitude, longing, or loss. Show one ordinary action instead of naming the emotion; avoid stock phrases such as “broken heart” or “forever yours”.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6, lesson-3. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) An action or object carries the feeling. (2) The language sounds specific to this speaker. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S3. Task, contrast and rubric D.

## 2. Meaning and narrative

### lesson-15 — Leave room for the listener

**Objective:** Use subtext and restraint.

**Principle:** Let spoken words and actions pull in different directions. Trust the reader to notice the gap, while keeping the situation clear.

**Original contrast:** “Of course I’m glad you’re going.” / She folded his map along the wrong road. Weaker evidence: A random gesture with no relationship to the farewell creates obscurity rather than subtext.

**Five-minute task:** Write four lines of a farewell. Let one gesture contradict the speaker’s words.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-14, lesson-7. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Speech and gesture imply a readable tension. (2) Restraint creates inference rather than mere vagueness. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S3, P18. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-symbol — Let an object change meaning

**Objective:** Develop a recurring object through changing context.

**Principle:** A repeated object becomes meaningful through what happens around it. Do not require a universal symbolic dictionary.

**Original contrast:** A gate first shelters a garden; later the same locked gate excludes a returning friend. Weaker evidence: Explaining “the gate symbolises loneliness” without any changed scene does not enact the development.

**Five-minute task:** Write two two-line moments using the same ordinary object with a changed relationship.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-7, lesson-15. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Contexts allow a reader to infer a changed meaning. (2) The object remains concrete and relevant. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D; related context in P18, not a consulted symbolism method. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-8 — A character at the threshold

**Objective:** Introduce character through choice.

**Principle:** An early action can reveal desire and a contradiction more efficiently than a biography. Give the person something to want now.

**Original contrast:** “The knight sold off his silver spurs / to buy his daughter dancing shoes.” Status gives way to affection. Weaker evidence: A list of age, clothing and eye colour supplies no revealing choice.

**Five-minute task:** Introduce a character in four lines. Include a desire and a revealing choice.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6, lesson-14. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) An action reveals both desire and character. (2) The reader can tell who acts and what matters. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P10; character-choice task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-9 — A reason to listen

**Objective:** Create conflict with a specific obstacle.

**Principle:** A story moves when a desire meets resistance. The obstacle should force a choice rather than merely delay the character.

**Original contrast:** A baker wants to feed a stranger, but the final loaf is promised to the mayor. Weaker evidence: A delay that never threatens the desire adds an event without consequential conflict.

**Five-minute task:** Give your previous character an obstacle. Write four lines ending just before a decision.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-8. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) An obstacle makes a choice necessary. (2) The conflict is understandable on one hearing. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P8; conflict task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-10 — The promise in the pocket

**Objective:** Plant a detail that can pay off later.

**Principle:** A setup quietly teaches the audience what to remember. A payoff changes what that detail means. Too much emphasis announces the ending.

**Original contrast:** Setup: a baker keeps a bent pin in her apron. Payoff: when the oven latch jams before the feast, that pin releases it. The small detail becomes a usable tool, rather than a convenient new invention. Weaker evidence: A key introduced only at the ending does not pay off an earlier setup.

**Five-minute task:** Write a two-line setup involving a small object. Add a two-line payoff that makes it matter.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-8, lesson-9. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The payoff uses an identifiable earlier detail. (2) The connection surprises without needing a new explanation. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D; no specific setup/payoff method established in these readings. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-11 — The road turns left

**Objective:** Subvert an established expectation.

**Principle:** A reversal works when the audience can follow the first expectation and recognise the second interpretation. Surprise needs enough logic to feel earned.

**Original contrast:** “He swore he feared no dragon’s roar. / The taxman knocked; he barred the door.” Weaker evidence: An unexpected ending with no connection to the established character is arbitrary.

**Five-minute task:** Write a four-line miniature ballad that establishes bravery and then reveals an unexpected fear.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-10. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The first expectation is established clearly. (2) The reversal is plausible within the miniature story. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D16; reversal task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-12 — Give the joke some air

**Objective:** Place the surprising word where it lands.

**Principle:** Timing on the page comes from ordering information. Put essential setup first and the surprising detail near the end. Extra explanation after a punchline often weakens it.

**Original contrast:** “The king requested honest counsel. / We sent a mirror. It was banished.” Weaker evidence: Explaining the surprise in another sentence can remove its intended landing.

**Five-minute task:** Write a two-line joke about an ordinary job. Revise so the surprise arrives in the last few words.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-11, lesson-3. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The setup supplies the information needed for the surprise. (2) The ending avoids explaining the joke after it lands. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D16; punchline-position task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-13 — Two meanings, one door

**Objective:** Use wordplay with a readable surface meaning.

**Principle:** A double meaning should support the scene on both readings. Avoid explaining the pun; make the situation do that work.

**Original contrast:** “The gardener said he needed space. / I moved the pots; he packed his case.” Space first suggests room for plants, then distance in a relationship. Weaker evidence: A second dictionary meaning that does not fit the scene is not a coherent pun.

**Five-minute task:** Write two lines about a profession using a word with two meanings. Keep both readings coherent.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-12, lesson-1. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Both meanings fit the scene. (2) The pun remains comprehensible without a footnote. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D16. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-16 — Words need somewhere to breathe

**Objective:** Fit a lyric to a repeatable phrase.

**Principle:** Speak or sing a simple phrase, then notice where you breathe. Important words should feel comfortable on strong musical moments. No audio equipment is needed.

**Original contrast:** Try “I kept a light beside the stair” over four taps, then change “a light” to “every little candle” and feel the crowding. Weaker evidence: Adding syllables until the important word must be rushed gives weak phrasing evidence.

**Five-minute task:** Hum or speak an original repeatable rhythmic phrase. Fit two lines to it, mark a breath/rest and document one word-stress choice.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-4, lesson-5, lesson-3. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The phrase can be spoken or sung without crowding important words. (2) A revision respects natural stress and breath. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S5, J1. Task, contrast and rubric D.

## 3. Lyric architecture and revision

### lesson-17 — A home for the chorus

**Objective:** Give verse and chorus different jobs.

**Principle:** Verses develop particulars; a chorus returns to the central idea. Repetition gains meaning when each verse changes the listener’s understanding.

**Original contrast:** Verse: a packed suitcase, an unpaid fare. Chorus: “There’s still a road that leads me home.” Weaker evidence: Repeating the verse detail without a larger returning idea gives the chorus no distinct job.

**Five-minute task:** Reuse four lines from an earlier scene or emotional exercise as a verse. Add a two-line chorus that names the larger idea, then explain in your reflection how the scene supports it.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6, lesson-14, lesson-16. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The verse gives particulars and the chorus a central idea. (2) The returning hook gains meaning from the verse. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S7. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-18 — Departure and return

**Objective:** Create and revise tension and release in language.

**Principle:** A question creates an expectation; a subsequent line can sharpen, postpone or answer it. This is a writing exercise. Chord function is taught and assessed separately in Music.

**Original contrast:** “Who kept the harbour light awake? / The keeper left; the light remained. / By dawn his daughter took the stair.” creates and then answers an implied question. Weaker evidence: Labeling three unrelated statements “tension/release” supplies no answer to a question.

**Five-minute task:** Write a question, a complication and a response in three short lines. Explain what changed. Musical accompaniment is optional and earns no theory credit.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-17, lesson-5. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The resolving line answers the earlier tension. (2) Musical claims remain optional without audio evidence. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P18; language-only adaptation D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-19 — A line made to be heard

**Objective:** Use emphasis and pauses to guide a listener.

**Principle:** Performance can change the meaning of the same words. Mark breaths and stress, then check that the audience can understand the story on one hearing.

**Original contrast:** “I never said / you stole the crown.” Stress I, said, or you to hear different implications. Weaker evidence: Capitalising every word identifies no meaningful contrast of emphasis.

**Five-minute task:** Choose four lines of your work. Mark pauses with / and key stresses with capitals. Perform two different readings.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-4, lesson-16. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Pause and emphasis choices serve the intended meaning. (2) The writer reflects on how another reading changes the effect. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F1, J3; performance self-report D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-20 — The verse earns its ending

**Objective:** Revise a complete verse with intention.

**Principle:** Revision is a sequence of decisions. Check meaning first, then image, rhythm, rhyme, and unnecessary words. Keep an imperfection when it serves the voice.

**Original contrast:** Before: “I waited in the freezing cold for you.” After: “Your last train left. I stayed beside the rail.” The revision makes the waiting visible. Which version works better depends on the speaker’s intention. Weaker evidence: Deleting words solely to reduce the count may damage the stated intention.

**Five-minute task:** Bring an earlier four-line verse. State its intention, revise two lines for clarity and concision, then read aloud once. Explain one choice you changed and one you kept.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6, lesson-2, lesson-5, lesson-14. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Two revisions serve the stated intention. (2) An explicit rationale weighs meaning, image, rhythm, and rhyme. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S10, D24. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-21 — One sound, several effects

**Objective:** Use alliteration and assonance to support meaning.

**Principle:** Alliteration repeats nearby initial consonant sounds; assonance repeats vowel sounds in nearby stressed syllables. Either can bind a phrase. Its effect depends on stress, pace, accent and context, so listen rather than assuming a sound always expresses one feeling.

**Original contrast:** Alliteration: “Tin cups clatter on the cart” repeats /k/. Assonance: “Slow boats roll beyond the coast” repeats /oʊ/ in many English accents. Speak both; your accent may differ. Weaker evidence: Sound repetition that makes the image unintelligible fails the chosen clarity goal.

**Five-minute task:** Rewrite one earlier image twice: once with repeated consonants, once with shared vowel sounds. Choose the version that fits your intent.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-6, lesson-4. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Sound repetition remains readable. (2) The writer explains a specific intended effect. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D17. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-22 — A shape to think inside

**Objective:** Draft an original four-line alternating-rhyme stanza.

**Principle:** A stanza is a unit of thought as well as sound. An ABAB scheme asks lines one and three, then two and four, to answer each other. Try a loose four-beat pulse before strict metre.

**Original contrast:** “The ferry bell outlived the light / A coat lay folded on the shore / One window kept its watch all night / Until the tide returned once more.” Weaker evidence: Choosing four rhyming endings without a connected thought supplies a scheme but not a stanza.

**Five-minute task:** Write four lines in ABAB with four strong beats per line. Reuse an image from “Something you can touch”; mark one place where speech resists the pattern.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-5, lesson-1, lesson-21. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The intended rhyme scheme is identifiable. (2) The writer recognises tension between metre and natural speech. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F3; equal four-beat ABAB task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-form-choice — Choose the shape

**Objective:** Compare a patterned stanza with deliberate freer lineation.

**Principle:** A line ending directs attention, while an end-stop or enjambment changes how syntax moves. Form is a choice, not a quality ranking.

**Original contrast:** “I left the key / beneath your apology” defers the object’s location; compare the single-line version. Weaker evidence: Inserting breaks every third word without an intended effect supplies arbitrary lineation.

**Five-minute task:** Recast two existing lines with two different break patterns; defend one. Optional extension: a complete constrained stanza.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-22. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Identifies an audible or semantic effect of a break. (2) Makes the chosen form serve the intended reading. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F1-II, F3; free-verse comparison D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-23 — A hook that means more

**Objective:** Create a concise title-hook whose meaning changes with context.

**Principle:** A hook is a memorable return, not merely a repeated phrase. Give a short title two plausible contexts, then let the verse earn one of them.

**Original contrast:** Title: “A little room.” A cramped apartment first; the freedom to forgive later. Weaker evidence: Repeating the same title after two interchangeable situations supplies no changed meaning.

**Five-minute task:** Choose a two-to-five-word title. Write two contrasting two-line verse sketches leading into that same title.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-17, lesson-15. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The title is concise and repeatable. (2) The contexts change its emotional meaning. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P7. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-24 — Keep the camera steady

**Objective:** Control speaker, addressee, and time.

**Principle:** A lyric has a viewpoint even when it does not name a narrator. Unsignalled changes of you, she, or tense can confuse a listener. Change them deliberately when the story needs a turn.

**Original contrast:** Confusing: “You left. She waits. I will be gone.” Clearer: “You left at dawn. I waited by the gate.” Weaker evidence: An unexplained switch from “you” to “she” makes the addressee uncertain.

**Five-minute task:** Take four earlier lines. Underline speaker, addressee, and time cues; revise one confusing shift or explain an intentional one.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-8, lesson-9. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Pronouns have understandable referents. (2) A time or perspective shift has a clear purpose. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P10, S10. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-ballad-scene — Make the next scene necessary

**Objective:** Sustain narrative causality in a ballad scene.

**Principle:** A story advances when an action changes what can happen next. A familiar ballad pulse can help, but is not compulsory.

**Original contrast:** A ferryman hides the fare; the passenger must offer the only message they carry. Weaker evidence: Three unrelated places mentioned in sequence give geography without causal development.

**Five-minute task:** Outline desire, obstacle and consequence; draft only the turning beat in two to four lines.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-10, lesson-24. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) A choice changes the next possible action. (2) Speaker, time and stakes remain understandable. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P8, F3-B; causal planning D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

## 4. Independent integration

### lesson-25 — Change the light

**Objective:** Create emotional contrast without contradicting the speaker.

**Principle:** Tender details can sharpen an angry verse; humour can make grief more human. Contrast becomes useful when both feelings arise from the same situation.

**Original contrast:** “I cursed your mug beside the sink, / then washed it carefully by hand.” Irritation and tenderness attach to the same object and opposing actions. Weaker evidence: Adding a cheerful line unrelated to the situation supplies contrast without emotional causality.

**Five-minute task:** Revise an earlier emotional stanza to contain a contrasting feeling. Keep the situation and speaker coherent.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-14, lesson-15, lesson-24. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Both feelings have concrete support. (2) The contrast deepens rather than obscures the central situation. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P18; mixed-emotion task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-26 — The joke belongs to someone

**Objective:** Build humour from character rather than a detached punchline.

**Principle:** A narrative joke becomes stronger when the reversal reveals a desire or blind spot already present in the character. Let consequences follow the setup.

**Original contrast:** “The mayor fined all idle talk. / By noon, his speech had bankrupted him.” The consequence reveals the same habit the character wants to punish. Weaker evidence: A detached pun that reveals nothing about the character misses the character-humour objective.

**Five-minute task:** Return to your character and obstacle. Write a four-line comic consequence using an earlier planted detail.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-10, lesson-11, lesson-12, lesson-13. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The surprise grows from an established character trait. (2) The payoff reuses a detail and remains legible. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D16. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-satire — Make the rule expose itself

**Objective:** Use contradiction to satirise a fictional behaviour or institution.

**Principle:** Satire has a target and a point of view. Incongruity can expose a rule whose consequences defeat its stated purpose; insults alone are not that mechanism.

**Original contrast:** A fictional silence committee hires a town crier to announce its bans. Weaker evidence: Calling every official foolish gives an opinion without showing the contradiction.

**Five-minute task:** Draft a four-line announcement whose practical consequence undermines its official aim.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-26. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The target and contradiction are legible. (2) The consequence grows from the established rule. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D16 for incongruity; this satire framework D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-27 — Make the bridge earn its place

**Objective:** Use a bridge to change perspective.

**Principle:** A bridge offers information or perspective that a repeated verse cannot. It can question the chorus, reveal a cost, or change the listener’s understanding before the return.

**Original contrast:** Chorus: “I always leave a light.” Bridge idea: the speaker realises the light is for themself. Weaker evidence: Restating the chorus in different words adds no new perspective.

**Five-minute task:** Write a two-line bridge for your earlier chorus. Add one sentence explaining how the chorus means something different afterwards.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-17, lesson-23, lesson-25. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The bridge adds a distinct perspective. (2) The returning chorus remains coherent but gains meaning. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** S8, S9; bridge task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-28 — Test on one hearing

**Objective:** Revise for a listener who cannot reread.

**Principle:** Reading aloud reveals breath, ambiguity, and crowded phrasing. A silent text review cannot measure actual delivery; record your own observations instead of asking AI to infer a performance.

**Original contrast:** “After Mira called Jo’s sister, she left.” The pronoun may confuse a first-time listener. Weaker evidence: “It sounds fine” without identifying a reading choice provides little usable evidence.

**Five-minute task:** Use an earlier verse and chorus, up to eight lines. Read them aloud once. Mark a stumble, a breath, or an unclear reference; if none occurs, name an emphasis choice. Revise one place and read again.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-19, lesson-24, lesson-16. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The self-report identifies a specific listening obstacle. (2) The revision improves clarity without flattening the voice. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** J1, D24; one-hearing protocol D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-29 — Revise for one intention

**Objective:** Evaluate several craft choices against a central purpose.

**Principle:** Prosody treats sound, structure, image, and language as choices serving the same emotional intention. Perfect regularity is not always the best choice for an unsettled speaker.

**Original contrast:** A speaker denying fear may use a steady refrain, then lose the rhyme at the moment of doubt. Weaker evidence: A list of edits with no link to the intention is an activity record, not an evaluation.

**Five-minute task:** Use a previous verse and chorus (up to eight lines). State the central intention. Spend three minutes revising one image and one rhythmic or rhyme choice; explain how both serve the intention and identify a deliberate irregularity you kept.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-20, lesson-22, lesson-25, lesson-27, lesson-28. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The intention is specific enough to guide decisions. (2) Two craft choices and one deliberate irregularity are justified by their effect rather than a rigid rule. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P18, D19. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-voice — Choose what sounds like you

**Objective:** Identify and vary personal stylistic decisions.

**Principle:** Voice emerges from repeated choices of detail, syntax, register and rhythm. It can develop; it is not a fixed personality score.

**Original contrast:** Compare “The rain got in again” with “The ceiling has resumed its argument”; choose the voice that fits this speaker. Weaker evidence: Using elaborate words merely to sound literary supplies no intentional voice.

**Five-minute task:** Rewrite two lines in two registers; name one habit to retain and one to test next.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-29. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Points to actual language choices and their effects. (2) Keeps a coherent speaker without rewarding ornament automatically. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** F4 for diction context; personal-voice protocol D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### lesson-30 — A small finished work

**Objective:** Transfer learned skills into an independently revised miniature.

**Principle:** A complete short piece can carry an arc: establish a situation, develop pressure, and leave a changed understanding. Completion is a considered decision, not proof of mastery.

**Original contrast:** Outline: a messenger guards a sealed letter; rain reveals blank paper; they choose what to say in their own voice. Weaker evidence: Stopping at the word limit does not establish that the ending answers the opening.

**Five-minute task:** Use a previous verse and chorus or short poem, up to twelve lines. Spend one minute identifying its opening promise, three revising its ending, and one naming a craft skill to practise next. Do not compose a new whole song in this session.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-29, lesson-26. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) The ending answers or deliberately reframes the opening. (2) A revision rationale names evidence and a next practice goal. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** P8, D24; transfer/capstone task D. Task, contrast and rubric D.

### writing-transfer — Carry the skill into unfamiliar work

**Objective:** Transfer two chosen craft skills to a new brief.

**Principle:** Independent transfer means selecting a useful tool when the prompt does not name it. An explanation and later revisit provide more evidence than a completion badge.

**Original contrast:** In a new miniature about repairing a kite, use a planted knot and a changed final image, then explain their relationship. Weaker evidence: Reusing the earlier poem with only the nouns replaced provides weak evidence of fresh decisions.

**Five-minute task:** Choose two skills, revise a new four-line miniature and name evidence for each. Portfolio comparison can take longer.

**Readiness / retrieval:** lesson-30, writing-voice. Retrieve the named skill before writing; revisit this attempt later with a changed subject.

**Assess:** (1) Applies two relevant skills without a step-by-step recipe. (2) Explains an effect and sets a specific next practice goal. The learner names one changed or deliberately retained choice and its intended effect.

**Source support:** D; revision context D24. Task, contrast and rubric D.

## Stage challenges and cross-faculty links

After stage 1, revisit an image while preserving conversational speech under a rhyme constraint. After stage 2, let a character's choice pay off an earlier object; the listener should understand the cause on one hearing. In stage 3, use lesson-20 and writing-ballad-scene to compare a local edit with a change in narrative consequence. Stage 4 uses lessons 29/30 and writing-transfer to combine craft and test independent choice on a fresh subject. These are revisits of listed units, not additional counted lessons.

After lesson-16, a learner may join the shared melody-to-lyrics studio once they can document a phrase. Lesson-4/5 support lyric-to-melody; lesson-25 supports harmony/meaning; lessons 17/27 support musical section contrast. The seven studios and both-faculty readiness criteria appear in [MUSIC_CURRICULUM.md](MUSIC_CURRICULUM.md). They do not block this Writing path.

## Remaining limits

The 30 current tasks are preserved as a review baseline; targeted clarifications above are proposed, not edits to installed content. The source-supported mechanisms are distinguished from original extensions, especially symbolism, satire framing, setup/payoff, voice and transfer. The supplied selection is stronger on lyrics and English verse than long-form storytelling or culturally diverse poetics. A teacher/learner pilot must check timing for lessons 17, 22, 29 and 30 and whether the new transfer task elicits independent choices. Editorial examples are not evidence of measured educational effectiveness.
